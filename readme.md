I. Title: Locking True Signals: Mitigating GNSS Spoofing with INS-Aided Tracking

II. Abstract: Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) provides absolute position, velocity, and time (PVT) solutions, serving as a key data source for the Internet of Things (IoT) multi-sensor fusion applications such as autonomous driving and precise timing. However, the openness of civilian GNSS signal systems and their lower reception power make them vulnerable to spoofing attacks—even in PVT systems integrated with Inertial Navigation Systems (INS), attackers can still control target receivers through spoofing signals.

Currently, research on GNSS spoofing is predominantly focused on "detection," with significant deficiencies in the development of rapid and effective "suppression technologies." To address this, this paper thoroughly analyzes the impact of spoofing attacks on tracking loops and proposes a robust Kalman filter-based suppression framework based on INS-assisted tracking (INSAT). This framework adaptively adjusts tracking parameters to suppress interference during spoofing pulls.

Experimental results using the Texas Electronic Testbed for Automated Spyware Testing (TEXBAT) software receiver demonstrate that with vehicle-level INS, INSAT can reduce the maximum position error from 600 meters to within 12 meters; experiments with self-collected datasets validate that under conditions where INS performance is sufficient, INSAT can maintain locking on true signals during spoofing pulls and recover corrections for INS biases after the spoofing ends, avoiding error accumulation in prolonged spoofing environments.
For the slow-speed deception traction scenario with an average speed of 0.5m/s, under the assistance of a vehicle-level Inertial Navigation System (INS), the maximum error of INSAT is only 20.3% lower than that of the Multi-Channel Deception Relief Algorithm (MC), 11.35% lower than that of the Loosely Coupled GNSS/INS Deception Relief Algorithm (LC), and 9.08% lower than that of the Tightly Coupled GNSS/INS Deception Relief Algorithm (TC). Moreover, this method does not require additional receivers, and its computational complexity is comparable to existing receivers, providing effective support for enhancing the integrity and security of Internet of Things applications.
However, GNSS has inherent vulnerabilities: attackers can send fake deception signals, severely interfering with the normal operation of Internet of Things systems. Such attacks are covert, leading to systemic errors and exacerbating security issues. Research has confirmed that deception attacks have significant negative impacts on fields such as autonomous driving, smart grids, civil aviation, and even animal behavior monitoring. Therefore, anti-deception technology has become an essential link in the development of the Internet of Things.
Regarding the strong disruptiveness and covertness of deception attacks, researchers have conducted extensive research on anti-deception methods. From an attack strategy perspective, deception can be divided into synchronous and asynchronous deception: the former requires precise synchronization with real signals, while the latter achieves penetration by adjusting code rate and sliding pseudorange, but both aim to align with the relevant peak of the real signal to complete deception traction.
In scenarios where some satellites are deceived, the "Detection - Identification - Exclusion (DRE)" strategy (combining detection and identification) can effectively suppress it — by distinguishing between real and deceptive signals, utilizing normal satellites for precise PVT solution. The core of DRE is detection, and the identification phase often relies on the consistency cross-check of multiple satellite channels, commonly using detection indicators including carrier-to-noise ratio (CNR), pseudorange residual, multipath consistency, and signal arrival angle (DOA). However, single parameter detection methods have high false alarm rates and narrow applicability, hence researchers propose a joint detection and relief scheme based on multi-parameter modeling:
Ali et al. combined ratio metrics with additional detectors for detection;
•  
Gross et al. integrated signal quality indicators (SQM) with signal power to enhance robustness against high and low power spoofing;
•  
The Stanford University GPS Laboratory proposed a joint framework based on Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (GLRT), which can integrate any number of indicators while maintaining a fixed maximum false alarm rate.
As the complexity of integrated indicators increases, the difficulty of framework construction also rises, leading to the emergence of machine learning-based methods. For example, Chen et al. proposed a multi-parameter detection method based on Support Vector Machine (SVM), extracting features such as SQM, CNR, Doppler, etc., achieving an accuracy over 90% in the TEXBAT dataset test.
However, with the upgrade of spoofing technology and the reduction of interference costs, portable full-band system spoofing devices continue to emerge, making DRE inapplicable to most spoofing scenarios. "True signal restoration" has become a research focus:
•  
Signal reconstruction: Reconstructing spoofed signals through parameters such as code delay, carrier phase, amplitude, and then subtracting from the original signal to suppress it, but requires continuous acquisition of parameters, making it complex;
•  
Multi-channel tracking: Integrating Multipath Estimation Delay Locked Loop (MEDLL) into the tracking loop to estimate all signal parameters to track both real and spoofed signals simultaneously, then grouping them according to consistency, but is difficult to identify the real signal group when lacking additional prior information. Rothmaier et al. once used INS state information to filter out real signals for decoding.
Information coupling is key to spoofing suppression, mainly including array antenna beamforming and multi-source fusion:
•  
Beamforming: Based on DOA detection to identify spoofed signals, forming nulls in the direction of the spoofed signal's incidence to achieve suppression, but array antennas are costly, large, difficult to deploy, and require attitude and positional prior information against multi-antenna spoofing, not suitable for Internet of Things applications;
1. Multi-source fusion: Fusing data from multiple sources such as INS, visual, 5G base stations, altimeters, and Lidar. For example, Chang has built a loosely coupled GNSS/INS/Lidar model for autonomous driving, while Chen has improved the chi-square detector in Kalman filtering, combined with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) to construct a GNSS/INS/Camera Pose Injection Mitigation Model, achieving precise positioning in pose attack scenarios.
1.2 Research gaps: Current multi-source fusion mitigation schemes using loose coupling involve integrating the output of GNSS receivers' position and velocity data with an autonomous navigation system, which responds slowly to cheat attacks; tight coupling methods extract pseudorange and Doppler shifts from GNSS receivers and combine them with INS state increments to build cheat detection indicators, significantly improving response speed and mitigation effectiveness. However, both methods do not incorporate INS's autonomous measurements into the tracking loop, failing to change the inherent characteristic of GNSS receivers "prioritizing high-power signal tracking" — during a cheat attack, the receiver will inevitably output incorrect measurement values.
Essentially, existing methods are still "upgraded DREs that quickly detect and discard GNSS observations," unable to fundamentally eliminate the presence of cheat signals; under prolonged cheat attacks, independent operation of INS can lead to significant error accumulation. To this end, this paper proposes an INS-assisted tracking (INSAT) method: During a cheat attack, INSAT can adaptively adjust tracking loop parameters to overcome the preference of the tracking loop for high-power signals, guiding it to lock onto the true signal, fundamentally avoiding the error accumulation issue of INS's independent operation under prolonged cheat attacks.
1.3 Research contributions: To effectively combat GNSS cheat interference and ensure the robustness and security of Internet of Things applications, this paper utilizes INS to assist GNSS receiver tracking, ensuring accurate tracking of real signals in a cheat environment, with main contributions including:
Propose an INSAT (Inertial Navigation Satellite) assisted tracking (INSAT) deception mitigation scheme to avoid long-term deception in INS error divergence;
2. 
Derive the impact of deception interference on tracking loops and design a robust Kalman filter for deception suppression that does not require additional sensors;
3. 
Test INSAT on the public dataset TEXBAT to verify that under vehicle-level INS assistance, this method can reduce the maximum positioning error by 98% under deception attacks;
4. 
Test the performance of INSAT under different deception traction speeds and power advantages, analyze the impact of traction speed and INS mass on deception suppression effects.
Conclusion: The core goal of this paper is to enhance the safety and integrity of GNSS systems in Internet of Things (IoT). Firstly, by analyzing the impact of deception attacks on tracking loops, an INSAT algorithm is developed based on a robust Kalman filter framework. Subsequently, the software receiver processes the TEXBAT dataset, experimentally confirming that vehicle-level inertial devices can reduce the maximum positioning error from 600 meters to within 12 meters.
Experiments with self-collected datasets further demonstrate that under conditions where INS performance is sufficient, INSAT can maintain lock on true signals during deception traction periods and recover corrections for INS deviations after deception traction ends, avoiding continuous error accumulation during prolonged deception environments. For the slow deception traction scenario with an average speed of 0.5m/s, under vehicle-level INS assistance, INSAT's maximum error is only 20.3% lower than that of the multi-channel deception mitigation algorithm (MC), 11.35% lower than the loosely coupled GNSS/INS deception mitigation algorithm (LC), and 9.08% lower than the tightly coupled GNSS/INS deception mitigation algorithm (TC).
The most significant contribution of this paper is that the proposed method can recover true signals in a deceptive environment without the use of multiple correlators, with computational complexity comparable to existing receivers, providing feasibility for deployment in IoT scenarios. It is expected to provide important technical support for GNSS security protection in key IoT applications such as autonomous driving and smart grids.
